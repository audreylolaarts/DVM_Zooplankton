The Calculations

Bennett McAfee's DaphniaDVM model, step by step: the equations that decide, for every size of Daphnia, which depth of the lake gives it the best chance of surviving the next six hours.

Every chart on this page is calculated live in your browser by the same code that drives the drawings, using McAfee's own example: Trout Lake in May 1992, at noon (full sun) and at midnight.

OverviewFrom lake to depth

The lake is divided into 2 m layers, from the surface to 32 m. Daphnia come in twelve sizes, from 5 to 60 μg. For every size in every layer the model works out two things: the chance of being eaten, and the energy gained or lost. It then looks ahead to see which layer leaves each size most likely to survive.

Step 1From weight to body length

The model tracks each Daphnia by its weight, x, in micrograms. Fish see length, not weight, so weight is converted to body length with a standard allometric relationship.

x

L

Step 2Light fades with depth

Sunlight at the surface, I0, is absorbed by the water as it goes down. Each layer keeps the same fraction of the light above it, so light falls off exponentially.

Step 3The chance of being eaten

Fish hunt by sight. How far away a fish can spot a Daphnia, its reactive distance, grows with the Daphnia's length and with the light, but levels off in bright light.

A fish swimming through the water sweeps out a cone of vision. Combined with how long it takes to catch and eat each prey, this gives a feeding rate per fish (a type II functional response, after Clark & Levy 1988):

Multiplying by the number of fish in the layer, then dividing by the number of prey, gives the chance that any one Daphnia is eaten during the time step:

Step 5Looking ahead: fitness

Each time step is 30 minutes. In a layer, a Daphnia either finds food, with probability λ, and ends up at weight x′, or finds none and ends up at x″. Below 5 μg it starves:

Its fitness, F, is its chance of still being alive at the end. That means not being eaten now, and then surviving from whatever weight it reaches. This is the heart of the model, a dynamic programming equation from Mangel & Clark (1988):

λ
probability of finding food, 0.5
chop
keeps weight between 5 and 60 μg; below 5 μg the Daphnia is dead
F(x, T)
at the final step, 1 if alive (above 5 μg), 0 if not

The model solves this backward, from the last of 12 time steps (six hours ahead) to the present. At each step, each size takes the best layer's fitness. Weights between the 5 μg classes are interpolated, so even small gains and losses count.

Step 6Choosing a depth

The best depth for each size is the layer with the highest fitness. Ties go to the shallower layer.

Step 7From depths to a population

Real Daphnia don't all sit at exactly one depth. McAfee turns each size's best depth into a bell curve with a standard deviation of 3.84 m, measured from field data in Sparkling Lake. The curves for all sizes are added together. The drawings do the same, weighting each size by how common it is in the Trout Lake samples.
